Executive summary

With a population of approximately 2.1 million, the Gaza Strip was already one of the world’s most densely populated places prior to the most recent period of conflict, which began on 7 October 2023. Since an October 2025 ceasefire agreement, up to 65% of Gazan territory has been under control of the Israeli military, resulting in a population density 2 to 3 times higher than its pre-conflict figure.

Gaza’s already-fragile economy and high levels of unemployment significantly worsened due to the conflict and almost 100% of the population now lives in poverty and requires humanitarian assistance. This is despite the ceasefire agreement, which took effect on 10 October 2025.

During the conflict, between 77% and 92% of Gaza’s residential units were damaged or destroyed. Up to 90% of the population continues to be displaced, living in overcrowded and sub-standard conditions. An accumulation of human waste in residential areas is attracting rodents and causing disease, particularly in children.

The food production and market infrastructure in Gaza collapsed due to the conflict. Most Gazans do not have access to the survival threshold of safe water and at least 59% of Gazans (1.2 million people) are experiencing acute food insecurity, at crisis level or worse. Food items on the market are unaffordable for most Gazans, rendering them reliant on food aid.

During the conflict, over 1,800 health facilities were completely or partially destroyed. Hospitals that continue to function do so under emergency conditions, without sufficient fuel, and with a severe medication shortage. Most Gazans remain without sufficient access to healthcare, and 20,000 patients need medical evacuation.

The aid effort continues to fall significantly short of meeting even basic survival needs. Insufficient aid is entering Gaza via the few border crossings, which are intermittently closed. Some essential goods are classified outside the scope of humanitarian aid and are denied entry or significantly delayed. Additional barriers to aid distribution include fuel shortages, road damage and debris, a lack of storage and distribution facilities, funding limitations, and ongoing security issues which have caused the loss of life to aid workers of a level described by the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights as ‘unprecedented’. There are also reports of Hamas diverting supplies arriving into Gaza, to sell to civilians at inflated prices.

The general humanitarian situation in Gaza is so severe that there are substantial grounds for believing there is a real risk of serious harm because conditions amount to torture or inhuman or degrading treatment as defined in paragraphs 339C and 339CA(iii) of the Immigration Rules/Article 3 of the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR).

Internal relocation is unlikely to be reasonable due to the overall shortfall of the arrival and distribution of humanitarian aid, the overcrowding and resulting extremely poor conditions in areas where aid is more easily distributed, and due to the moving boundary of the areas under Israeli military control and the surrounding buffer zone.

Where a claim is refused, it is unlikely to be certifiable as ‘clearly unfounded’ under section 94 of the Nationality, Immigration and Asylum Act 2002.

Each case must be considered on its individual facts. The onus is on the person to demonstrate they face persecution or serious harm.

About the assessment

This section considers relevant evidence – including country information, refugee and human rights law and policy, and case law – to assess whether, in general:

  • the humanitarian situation is so severe that there are substantial grounds for believing that there is a real risk of serious harm because conditions amount to inhuman or degrading treatment as within paragraphs 339C and 339CA(iii) of the Immigration Rules/Article 3 of the European Convention on Human Rights
  • internal relocation is possible to avoid serious harm
  • if refused, a claim is likely or not to be certified as ‘clearly unfounded’ under section 94 of the Nationality, Immigration and Asylum Act 2002.

Some COI sources cited in this note refer to Gaza as an Occupied Palestinian Territory (OPT). It should be noted, however, that on 21 September 2025, the UK made an announcement of its recognition of Palestine as a state. Therefore, all CPIT references in this note refer to ‘Palestine’. However, it should also be noted that Palestine is not universally recognised as a state and, as such, some COI sources may continue to refer to Palestine as an OPT. It should also be noted that the sources cited within this document use a variety of terms to refer to the Gaza Strip in its entirety, including ‘Gaza’ and ‘the Strip’. 2. Exclusion

2.1 General approach to exclusion

2.1.1 Decision makers must consider whether there are serious reasons to apply one (or more) of the exclusion clauses. Each case must be considered on its individual facts.

2.2 Applicability of Article 1D of the Refugee Convention

2.2.1 Article 1D of the Refugee Convention is one of the exclusion clauses in the Refugee Convention. It excludes persons receiving protection or assistance from organs or agencies of the United Nations (other than UNHCR) from the Refugee Convention. However, its overall purpose is to ensure the continuing protection of Palestinian refugees until their position is settled in accordance with relevant United Nations General Assembly resolutions.

2.2.2 Palestinian refugees in the Gaza Strip who were previously assisted by the United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East (UNRWA) and continue to be eligible for such assistance but who seek asylum outside the area of UNRWA operation are excluded from the scope of the Refugee Convention unless they can show that UNRWA assistance or protection has ceased for any reason. This includes where a person ceases to receive protection or assistance beyond their control or independent of their volition.

2.2.3 A Palestinian eligible for UNRWA protection or assistance and previously registered with UNRWA, or (though not registered) in receipt of UNRWA protection or assistance, is not entitled to Refugee Convention refugee status simply by leaving the UNRWA areas of operation and claiming asylum elsewhere.

2.2.4 Situations where UNRWA protection or assistance may cease beyond the person’s control, or not of their own volition, may include the following circumstances:

  • where there is a threat to life, physical integrity or security or freedom, or other serious protection related reasons
  • situations such as armed conflict or other situations of serious violence, unrest and insecurity, or events seriously disturbing public order
  • more individualised threats or protection risks such as sexual and/or gender-based violence, human trafficking and exploitation, torture, inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment, severe discrimination
  • arbitrary arrest or detention

2.2.5 Additionally, practical legal and/or safety barriers to accessing UNRWA assistance may mean that UNRWA assistance is in practice no longer available and may include:

  • being unable to access UNRWA assistance because of long-term border closures, roadblocks or closed transport routes
  • absence of documentation to travel to, or transit, or to re-enter and reside, or where the authorities in the receiving country refuse their re-admission or the renewal of their travel documents
  • serious dangers such as minefields, factional fighting, shifting war fronts, banditry or a real risk of other forms of violence or exploitation

2.2.6 In the non-binding case of SN and LN v Zamestnik-predsedatel na Darzhavna agentsia za bezhantsite (Directive 2011/95/EU – Person registered with UNRWA), heard on 11 January 2024 and promulgated on 13 June 2024, the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) considered whether, within the meaning of EU Directive 2011/95, UNRWA protection or assistance has ceased when UNRWA finds itself unable to ensure dignified living conditions for the person registered with, or eligible for assistance from, UNRWA, for whatever reason, including by reason of the general situation prevailing in an UNRWA area of operation.

2.2.7 The CJEU held in SN and LN that UNRWA assistance must be considered to have ceased in an UNRWA area of operation when:

‘… (i) that body [UNRWA] finds itself unable, for whatever reason, including by reason of the general situation in the sector of that body's area of operations, in which that stateless person had his or her habitual residence, to ensure to that stateless person, taking into account, where applicable, his or her state of vulnerability, dignified living conditions, consistent with its mission, without him or her being required to demonstrate that he or she is specifically targeted by that general situation by reason of elements specific to his or her personal situation, and

‘(ii) that stateless person of Palestinian origin would find himself or herself, if he or she were to return to that sector, in a state of serious insecurity, taking into account, where applicable, his or her state of vulnerability, since the administrative and judicial authorities are required to carry out an individual assessment of each application for international protection based on that provision, within the framework of which the age of the person concerned may be relevant.’ (Closing paragraph 2)

2.2.8 In summary, the CJEU held in SN and LN that UNRWA's assistance or protection must be considered to have ceased to a person registered with UNRWA, or eligible for UNRWA assistance, when UNRWA is no longer able to provide, within its relevant area of operation: dignified living conditions, and minimum security conditions.

2.2.9 The CJEU also held in SN and LN that the assessment of whether UNRWA’s protection or assistance has ceased in the relevant UNRWA area of operation should take into consideration the period of time from which the person left the UNRWA area of operation until the time of the protection determination.

2.2.10 While the UK is not bound by the CJEU determination in SN and LN, the judgement provides helpful guidance on factors to be taken into consideration within an assessment of whether UNRWA protection or assistance has ceased for any reason within the meaning of Article 1D of the Refugee Convention. As such, decision makers should take the CJEU judgement of SN and LN into account when making this assessment.

2.2.11 Palestinians resident in Gaza who were not receiving or eligible to receive protection or assistance from UNRWA are not excluded under Article 1D. These cases should be considered on their merits under the Refugee Convention unless there are serious reasons for considering whether one (or more) of the other exclusion clauses is applicable. Each case must be considered on its individual facts and merits.

2.2.12 Further guidance on handling Palestinians assisted by UNRWA is set out in the Asylum Instruction on Article 1D of the Refugee Convention: Palestinian refugees assisted by the United Nations Relief and Works Agency (UNRWA) (see also Points to note).

2.3 Exclusion under Article 1F of the Refugee Convention

2.3.1 There are a number of armed groups operating in Gaza, including (but not limited to) the military wings of Palestinian groups such as Hamas (Harakat al-Muqawama al-Islamiya, “Islamic Resistance Movement”) and the Palestinian Islamic Jihad (PIJ). Some of these groups may be involved in terrorist activities (several are proscribed under the UK Terrorism Act 2000, see Proscribed terrorist groups or organisations) or are responsible for serious human rights abuses in the Gaza Strip as well as in neighbouring countries such as Israel and other areas of Palestine.

2.3.2 If there are serious reasons for considering that the person has been involved with these groups, then decision makers must consider whether any of the exclusion clauses under Article 1F are applicable.

2.3.3 If the person is excluded from the Refugee Convention under Article 1F, they will also be excluded from a grant of humanitarian protection, which has a wider range of exclusions than refugee status.

2.3.4 For guidance on exclusion and restricted leave, see the Asylum Instruction on Exclusion under Articles 1F and 33(2) of the Refugee Convention, Humanitarian Protection and the instruction on Restricted Leave.

3. Convention reason(s)

3.1.1 A severe humanitarian situation does not in itself give rise to a well-founded fear of persecution for a Refugee Convention reason.

3.1.2 In the absence of a link to one of the 5 Refugee Convention grounds necessary to be recognised as a refugee, the question is whether the person will face a real risk of serious harm in order to qualify for Humanitarian Protection (HP).

3.1.3 However, before considering whether a person requires protection because of the general humanitarian situation, decision makers must consider if the person faces persecution for a Refugee Convention reason. Where the person qualifies for protection under the Refugee Convention, decision makers do not need to consider if there are substantial grounds for believing the person faces a real risk of serious harm meriting a grant of HP.

3.1.4 For further guidance on the 5 Refugee Convention grounds, see the Asylum Instruction, Assessing Credibility and Refugee Status. For guidance on HP see the Asylum Instruction, Humanitarian Protection.

4. Risk

4.1.1 The general humanitarian situation in Gaza is so severe that there are substantial grounds for believing that there is a real risk of serious harm because conditions amount to torture or inhuman or degrading treatment as defined in paragraphs 339C and 339CA(iii) of the Immigration Rules/Article 3 of the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR). Each case must be considered on its individual facts.

4.1.2 With a population of approximately 2.1 to 2.2 million, the Gaza Strip was already one of the world’s most densely populated places prior to the most recent period of conflict, which began on 7 October 2023. Since the October 2025 ceasefire agreement, however, between 54% and 65% of Gazan territory has been under control of the Israeli military, resulting in a population density 2 to 3 times higher than its pre-conflict figure (see Geography and demography).

4.1.3 Gaza’s economy contracted to approximately 18% of its pre-conflict size by October 2025, while unemployment rates rose from approximately 45%, already among the highest in the world, to approximately 78% as of December 2025. Sources report, 9 months into the ceasefire, that any significant recovery of the economy and livelihoods is yet to begin. In late July 2026, the World Food Programme reported a further ‘collapse’ of the economy and a rise in the unemployment rate, to 81%. The number of Gazans living in poverty increased from approximately 64% pre-conflict to almost 100% by May 2024, which prevailed as of July 2026. Sources also report between 90% and 100% of the population continues to require humanitarian assistance despite the October 2025 ceasefire, while humanitarian needs continue to be described as ‘unprecedented’ and ‘dire’ (see Socio-economic situation, Poverty and People in need (PIN)).

4.1.4 By October 2025, sources report between 77% and 92% of Gaza’s (c.320,000 and 372,000) housing/residential units have been damaged or destroyed. The conflict caused the displacement of up to 90% of the population, who have mostly remained displaced since the ceasefire. Shelter needs are mostly unmet, living conditions are overcrowded and substandard, and the situation is intermittently worsened by weather events such as the cold of winter, the high temperatures of the summer, and flooding caused by heavy rains impacting makeshift shelters which include tents. Most displacement sites are on privately owned land and 93% have no formal tenure. According to the UN Human Rights Council, women, children, people with disabilities and older people are disproportionately affected by the loss of housing and displacement, including the resulting homelessness (see Shelter and non-food items (NFI)).

4.1.5 Most of Gaza’s wastewater treatment plants and sewage pumping stations were rendered inoperable by the conflict. While access to water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) facilities has improved since the ceasefire, access to safe water continues to be limited, and most Gazans do not have access to sufficient water and sanitation. A global NGO reported water production in Gaza of approximately 6 litres per person per day in July 2026, which it stated was 40% of its pre-conflict level of production and significantly below the WHO’s recommended minimum of 15 litres per person per day (and its absolute survival threshold of 7.5 litres pppd used in emergency planning). Furthermore, UNICEF reported in its mid-year 2026 analysis, that 70% of families are unable to access 6 litres per person per day. The accumulation of human waste in residential areas, which approximately 60% of the population is reportedly exposed to, is attracting pests and rodents. Despite a pest control campaign, this, and the other WASH issues in the Gaza Strip, are resulting in diseases, particularly in children, including skin diseases and infections, diarrhoea, and hepatitis A. The Norwegian Refugee Council reports that children, older people, people with chronic illnesses, and women and girls face high risks of the adverse affects of the WASH issues in the Gaza Strip (see Water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH)).

4.1.6 The conflict resulted in the collapse of the food production and market infrastructure. As of October 2025, more than 95% of agriculture infrastructure was destroyed and 96% of cropland could no longer be cultivated, due to damage and/or inaccessibility. The Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC), which had declared famine in the Gaza governorate in August 2025, no longer classified any area of Gaza as being in famine by December 2025. It did, however, consider this progress to be extremely fragile, with approximately 77% of the population (1.6 million Gazans) continuing to face high levels of acute food insecurity. By July 2026, the IPC classified 59% of the population (over 1.2 million Gazans) as facing crisis or worse conditions, including 10% of the population (212,000 people) in emergency conditions, with all five governorates classified as being in the crisis phase. While the price of food items, which skyrocketed during the conflict, has decreased again since the ceasefire, they remain higher than pre-conflict prices such that food items continue to be unaffordable for most Gazans, rendering them reliant on food aid (see Food security).

4.1.7 Children are among the most impacted and vulnerable people in Gaza; more than 58,000 children lost one or both parents and virtually all children are reported to require mental health and psychosocial support due to the conflict. As of early 2026, approximately 100,000 children remained acutely malnourished, 10 children had died due to freezing temperatures during a winter in makeshift shelters, and child injuries and fatalities due to climbing on moving trucks to obtain supplies were reported in 11 Rafah and Khan Younis locations. As of June 2026, children continued to be at direct risk of dehydration and waterborne disease due to the WASH situation and there were repeated reports of children being bitten by rodents while they slept. More children aged 6 months to 5 years (3,000) were admitted for malnutrition treatment in June 2026 than were in May 2026 (2,800), and 70% of children still do not reach minimum dietary diversity. An estimated 728,000 school-aged children have missed more than 2 years of formal schooling while more than 97% of schools and almost all university buildings were damaged or destroyed. While access to education has improved in recent months, as of July 2026, over 637,000 children still had no sustained access to in-person learning according to a collective of actors working in the education sector, led by UNICEF and Save the Children, and security-related incidents in temporary education settings continue to occur despite the ceasefire (see Children and Education).

4.1.8 During the conflict, over 1,800 health facilities in the Gaza Strip were completely or partially destroyed, over 43,000 Gazans sustained life-changing injuries, and, as of February 2026, more than 6,000 amputation cases had been reported. While the number of functioning health services has increased since the October 2025 ceasefire, many hospitals operate under permanent emergency conditions, without sufficient fuel or medicines. Most of the population remains without sufficient access to healthcare. A non-profit Palestinian research institution reported that as of 7 July 2026, none of Gaza’s hospitals were fully functional, 20 were partially functional, and 17 were non-functioning. A global NGO reported there to be c.43% fewer hospital beds (2,000) post-conflict compared with pre-conflict (3,500). Shortages of essential medical supplies continue to hamper non-contagious disease services. Diabetes and hypertension patients, who numbered 80,000 and 110,000 respectively, have no medicine available to them, while 47% of essential medicines, and 64% of cancer medicines, were also at ‘zero stock’, according to the mentioned research institution. According to the UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA), a total of 2,107 patients were medically evacuated from the Gaza Strip for treatment abroad between January 2026 and 28 July 2026. However, sources report that 1,400 patients died between May 2024 and March 2026 while awaiting medical evacuation, and that a further 20,000 Gazans remain in need of medical evacuation. Over 1,700 health workers have been killed as of 29 July 2026 (see Health and healthcare).

4.1.9 The United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East (UNRWA) remained the largest provider of humanitarian assistance in Gaza despite funding difficulties and several announcements by UNRWA that it was no longer able to provide humanitarian assistance (see the previous version of this CPIN (v4.0, November 2024). However, amendments to Israeli legislation, effective from January and December 2025, effectively banned UNRWA activities on Israeli territory, severed contact between Israeli government staff and UNRWA, and prohibited authorised suppliers of essential utilities from providing services to UNRWA (or people acting on its behalf), among other provisions. Despite this, approximately 11,000 UNRWA personnel already inside the Gaza Strip have continued to provide humanitarian assistance to its population including emergency shelter, healthcare, psychosocial support, education, and WASH services. While UNRWA had pre-positioned supplies outside of Gaza, none of it has entered the Strip since 2 March 2025 and its flour and food parcels inside Gaza ran out ‘several months’ before July 2026 (see UN Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East (UNRWA)).

4.1.10 Other UN agencies, several States, NGOs, and multiple international organisations, as well as local communities and Palestinian civil society organisations have all undertaken humanitarian aid operations in Gaza. New Israeli regulations for international NGOs, which became effective on 1 January 2026, resulted in the suspension of 37 humanitarian organisations. While suspended organisations were no longer able to bring in aid from outside Gaza, they were able to continue their work using supplies obtained from within Gaza. While Israel insisted that the affected organisations made a minimal impact, a humanitarian news agency reported the dismantling of the aid system. As of late July 2026, 393 UNRWA/UNRWA-affiliated, and 203 non-UNRWA aid workers, had been killed since the start of the hostilities (see Other aid agencies and United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East (UNRWA)).

4.1.11 The entry of humanitarian aid into the Gaza Strip has, overall, increased significantly since the October 2025 ceasefire. However, the number of aid trucks entering Gaza fluctuates significantly, and sources state that the aid effort continues to fall significantly short of meeting even basic survival needs. The few border crossings are intermittently closed. At the time of writing, only one crossing point (Kerem Shalom) has been operational since late May 2026. Some essential goods are classified outside the scope of humanitarian aid and are denied entry or significantly delayed. Additional barriers to the distribution of aid include fuel shortages, road damage and debris, a lack of storage and distribution facilities, funding limitations, and ongoing security issues which have caused the loss of life to aid workers of a level described by the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights as ‘unprecedented’. There are also reports of Hamas diverting arriving supplies in the Gaza Strip, to sell to civilians at inflated prices (see Access to humanitarian aid).

4.1.12 In the country guidance case of HS (Palestinian – return to Gaza) Palestinian Territories CG [2011] UKUT 124 (IAC), heard on the 15 and 16 December 2009, 22 and 23 February 2010, and 10 June 2010, and promulgated 11 April 2011, the Upper Tribunal (UT) considered, amongst other matters, whether the general situation in Gaza amounted to a breach of Article 3 of the ECHR. The UT looked at a wide range of evidence primarily covering events in 2008 and 2009 after the Israeli blockade had begun and large-scale conflict occurred during ‘Operation Cast Lead’. The hostilities led to thousands of casualties as well as substantial damage to infrastructure, economic activity and public services (see paras 186 to 214).

4.1.13 On the basis of the evidence before it, the UT held in HS that:

‘Our assessment of the background evidence is that it clearly shows a harsh state of affairs in Gaza which reflects a deterioration beyond the situation prior to the Operation Cast Lead hostilities. The infrastructure of Gaza is significantly depleted, and there are problems of access to electricity and clean water and there are limits on the amount of products that are brought into the territory. We do not seek to undervalue the level of difficulty that the appellants in this case, and indeed other residents of Gaza, face in the territory. But we consider that the tests set out in the Refugee Convention as applied in the case law and under Article 3 are set at a level of risk which is higher than that which would be experienced by the appellant and her family in this case on return …

‘As regards the general socio-economic and humanitarian situation in Gaza, there is on the whole common ground in the evidence provided by both sides, although some of the evidence on the part of the Secretary of State indicates some small level of improvement in various respects. There has to be shown to be a severe deprivation with denial of shelter, food and the most basic necessities of life for the appeal to succeed. It is relevant to note … that to succeed in a claim for protection based on poor socio-economic or dire humanitarian living conditions under … Article 15 of the Qualification Directive or Article 3, the circumstances would have to be extremely unusual … The appellant and her family have relatives in Gaza, and, even if they are unable to accommodate them, they have friends also, and there is a good deal of humanitarian aid … It is necessary to bear in mind the reduced levels of violence, and the fact that basic goods are, to a limited extent, being imported into Gaza whether with Israeli assistance or as a consequence of being brought in through the tunnels, and though the situation is a serious one, we do not consider that it crosses the Article 3 or Refugee Convention threshold…

‘The conditions in Gaza are not such as to amount to persecution or breach of the human rights of returnees or place them in need of international protection’ (paragraphs 222, 224 and 225(6)).

4.1.14 However, a subsequent determination by the Court of Appeal (EWCA) in the case of MI (Palestine) v Secretary of State for the Home Department [2018] EWCA Civ 1782, heard on 19 July 2018, promulgated on 31 July 2018, considering arguments about the Article 3 threshold following the European Court of Human Rights case of Sufi and Elmi v. The United Kingdom - 8319/07 [2011] ECHR 1045 (28 June 2011) held that it is:

‘… sufficiently arguable that the conditions in Gaza are and were attributable to the direct and indirect actions of the parties to the conflict within the meaning of [282] of Sufi & Elmi and that there was an element of intentionality if that is a necessary ingredient before the approach in that case will be adopted …. Whether the case is one to which the Sufi & Elmi approach should apply will be a matter for that [UT] to decide.

‘Finally, our attention was drawn to the fact that the Country Guidance in HS not only pre-dates the decision in Sufi & Elmi but is also dealing with the position as it was up to 2010, some years before the 2014 military operation with its serious impact on the population and the infrastructure. Counsel suggested that perhaps a new Country Guidance case on Gaza should be considered. Ultimately that is a matter for the Upper Tribunal, not this Court, although I can see the sense of the suggestion given that, on any view the Country Guidance in HS is somewhat out of date.’ (paras 32 to 34)

4.1.15 At the time of writing, there has not been a CG case on Gaza since HS. However, humanitarian conditions in Gaza have significantly and durably deteriorated since HS was promulgated. The country information in this note indicates that there are ‘very strong grounds supported by cogent evidence’ to depart from these findings.

4.1.16 For further guidance on assessing risk, see the Asylum Instruction on Assessing Credibility and Refugee Status and, for guidance on HP see the Asylum Instruction, Humanitarian Protection.

One of the three main seawater desalination plants and two of three bulk water supply connections are non-functional, severely constraining the Gaza Strip’s largest sources of safe water. Stormwater infrastructure has also been heavily impacted, with 88 percent of pumping stations damaged and basins increasingly contaminated with untreated sewage. Remote sensing-based damage assessments indicate that, while portions of underground networks may remain physically intact, their usability will depend on extensive field verification and hydraulic modeling once access permits.

‘… The destruction of WASH services has had severe human and cross-sectoral impacts. Only a small fraction of the population now has access to piped drinking water, while the absence of wastewater treatment and solid waste services has sharply increased risks of waterborne disease, flooding, and environmental contamination. Women and girls face disproportionate burdens related to domestic water use, with hundreds of thousands lacking adequate supplies and privacy. Health facilities, displacement sites, schools, commercial locations, and agricultural lands are operating under critical WASH constraints, undermining health outcomes, protection, and human dignity.’[footnote 123]

10.5 Food security

10.5.1 The joint World Bank Group, EU and UN RDNA report stated, that as of October 2025:

‘The conflict has resulted in the collapse of the food production and market infrastructure; damages are estimated at US$1.44 billion [approximately £1.07 billion GBP[footnote 146]]. More than 95 percent of agriculture infrastructure including cropland, orchards, greenhouses, nurseries, irrigation networks and wells, agricultural roads, storage and processing facilities, markets and bakeries, solar installations, fisheries assets, and landing-site infrastructure have been destroyed. In addition, 96 percent of cropland was damaged, inaccessible, or both, with only a small share remaining cultivable even after ceasefire-related access changes — and on which currently most of the population is residing in displacement. This structural collapse has effectively halted agriculture at scale, disrupted value chains across crops, livestock, and fisheries, and fragmented formal markets, pushing traders into informal channels and forcing many households, particularly women and children, to revert to subsistence-level coping strategies.’[footnote 147]

10.5.2 The RDNA report also stated:

‘Prices in Gaza have experienced exceptional fluctuations over the last two years. Consumer price inflation (CPI) increased by 238 percent y-o-y in 2024, driven by food and transport costs. After the first ceasefire started in January 2025, and the increase in the amounts of aid supplies and food allowed into Gaza, CPI registered a decline for two consecutive months. With the end of the ceasefire in March 2025, hostilities resumed, and restrictions were reimposed on the movement of goods through commercial crossings. As a result, prices skyrocketed again until the recent ceasefire took effect in October 2025, when prices started going down.’[footnote 148]
