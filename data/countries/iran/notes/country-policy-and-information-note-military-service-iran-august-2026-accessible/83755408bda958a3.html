Executive summary

Military service is compulsory for all males aged between 18 and 49 and lasts for between 14 and 24 months. Some exemptions apply, on both non-medical and medical grounds, including for gay men and transgender people, who are classified as having a mental disorder in Iran.

Specific military training is limited to 2 months for most conscripts. Soldiers performing their duties are responsible for operational areas, deprived regions, harsh climates, and border security. Conditions for conscripts vary with the type and place of deployment, though salaries are generally relatively low, and humiliation, restrictions over leave, and poor sanitation during military training have all been reported.

A person who evades military service by leaving the country must complete their service on return if they are under the age of 40. For those over 40, a fine is the most common punishment. Draft evaders may also lose social benefits and civil rights including access to employment, higher education, and the ability to leave Iran.

The Iranian government has announced several regulations which pardon some deserters. However, those who remain liable for prosecution may face imprisonment.

Combatants who desert from the front line, causing the Islamic front to fail or resulting in casualties to Iranian forces, may be prosecuted under a charge that can invoke, at the judge’s discretion, the death penalty. However, only one example could be found of a member of military personnel having been sentenced to death since 2023. The arrest and sentencing, for refusing a military order, took place during a period of nationwide unrest.

Draft evaders or deserters are unlikely to fall within the scope of the Refugee Convention by reason of political opinion, race, religion, nationality, or as a particular social group (PSG). Neither are they likely to be subjected to a punishment that, including by virtue of its nature or repetition, amounts to persecution or serious harm.

In general, conscripts are unlikely to be required to commit acts that are contrary to the basic rules of human conduct, and unlikely to face conditions and/or treatment while serving their military service that would, including by virtue of its nature or repetition, be so harsh as to amount to a real risk of persecution or serious harm.

Where the person has a well-founded fear of persecution or serious harm from the state, they are unlikely to be able to obtain protection or to be able to internally relocate to escape that risk.

Where a claim is refused, it is unlikely to be certifiable as ‘clearly unfounded’ under section 94 of the Nationality, Immigration and Asylum Act 2002.

Each case must be considered on its individual facts. The onus is on the person to demonstrate they face persecution or serious harm.

  • a person faces a real risk of persecution or serious harm by the state because:
    • military service would involve acts, with which the person may be associated, which are contrary to the basic rules of human conduct

3. Convention reason(s)

3.1.1 Draft evaders or deserters are unlikely to fall within the scope of the Refugee Convention by reason of political opinion, race, religion or nationality.

3.1.2 Draft evaders or deserters in Iran are also not considered to form a particular social group (PSG) within the meaning of the Refugee Convention. This is because they do not share an innate characteristic, or a common background that cannot be changed, or share a characteristic or belief that is so fundamental to identity or conscience that a person should not be forced to renounce it, and they do not have a distinct identity in Iran because the group is not perceived as being different by the surrounding society.

3.1.3 A person refusing to do military service, including as a conscientious objector, may claim to do so due to their religious or political beliefs. However, the Asylum Instruction on Military Service and Conscientious Objection and paragraph 22 of the House of Lords judgement in the case of Sepet & Another v. SSHD [2003] UKHL 15 explain that it is necessary to carefully examine the reason for persecution in the mind of the persecutor, rather than the reason for persecution as it is believed by the victim.

3.1.4 There is limited evidence to suggest that the Iranian government generally views a refusal to undertake military service as an act of political or religious disobedience. Therefore, a person, even a conscientious objector, who evades or deserts military service is unlikely to be able to establish that their claim falls within the scope of the Refugee Convention. However, an evader or deserter who also belongs to a minority ethnic group, for example, a Kurd, may be able to establish that the Iranian government views their evasion or desertion as a sign of disloyalty or distrust of the state, and as such, as a political opponent (see Draft evasion and desertion). In such cases, the convention reason will be race and/or actual or imputed political opinion.

3.1.5 In the absence of a link to one of the 5 Refugee Convention reasons necessary for the grant of asylum, the question is whether the person will face a real risk of serious harm to qualify for Humanitarian Protection (HP).

3.1.6 Establishing a convention reason is not sufficient to be recognised as a refugee. The question is whether the person has a well-founded fear of persecution on account of an actual or imputed Refugee Convention reason.

3.1.7 For further guidance on the 5 Refugee Convention grounds, see the Asylum Instruction, Assessing Credibility and Refugee Status.

4. Risk

4.1 General approach to claims based on (compulsory) military service

4.1.1 Compulsory national service is a prerogative of sovereign states. A requirement to do compulsory military service – or punishment for failing to complete this duty – does not, in itself, give rise to a well-founded fear of persecution. It will only do so where, on account of a Convention reason:

  • military service would involve acts, with which the person may be associated, which are contrary to the basic rules of human conduct, or
  • the conditions of military service would be so harsh as to amount to persecution, or
  • the punishment for draft evasion or desertion is disproportionately harsh or severe.

4.2 Requirements and exemptions

4.2.1 Military service is compulsory for all males aged between 18 and 49. It lasts for between 14 and 24 months (see Eligibility and Duration).

4.2.2 Conscientious objection is not possible. However, some exemptions exist:

a. on medical grounds, for men with physical or mental illnesses; or

b. on non-medical grounds, for (i) students, (ii) men with specified family members who serve/have served in the military including those who have been killed or gone missing during the course of their service, and (iii) men deemed responsible for elderly or disabled family members, or multiple children, under specified circumstances (see Exemptions); or

c. for gay men and transgender people, because they are classified in Iran as having a mental disorder (see Exemptions: Gender and sexual orientation and the Country Policy and Information Note, Iran: Sexual orientation and gender identity and expression).

4.2.3 For more on assessing risk, see the Instructions Assessing Credibility and Refugee Status and Military Service and Conscientious Objection.

4.3 Acts contrary to the basic rules of human conduct

4.3.1 Conscripts are, in general, unlikely to be required to commit acts that are contrary to the basic rules of human conduct. The onus is on the person to demonstrate otherwise.

4.3.2 Decision makers should consider that the Iranian authorities are likely to intensify their requirements of conscripts, and/or their response to conscripts who refuse to carry out orders, during periods of unrest in Iran, which may include national civil unrest or international conflict.

4.3.3 Sources indicate that specific military training is limited to 2 months for most conscripts. Soldiers performing duties are responsible for operational areas, deprived regions, harsh climates, and border security, with most conscripts enlisted into the traditional Army. While the Australian Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade reported that conscripts cannot choose their type or location of service, educated conscripts mostly serve in desk jobs having applied to a government organisation of their choice, and skilled conscripts may be assigned to units suited to their skills (see Deployment and duties).

4.3.4 Most recruits to the IRGC serve there through choice, often deployed after a period of active Basij (the IRGC’s voluntary civil militia) membership. A maximum of 20% of draftees with a high school diploma, or less, may be involuntarily assigned to IRGC roles in underprivileged and poorer areas. Some conscripts are assigned to national police units, which may reportedly be used to suppress protests during times of national unrest. One Kurdish news agency reported that the deployment of Kurdish conscripts, particularly those who are Sunni or follow another minority religion, is more restrictive than for non-Kurds in respect of both the assigned role and its location. However, in the example provided by the source, the Kurdish conscript was able to opt to extend his service time rather than partake in armed activities. In times of war or general mobilisation all conscripted personnel are required by law to serve at least one year of their service in operational areas, however, there is no evidence this requirement is exercised in practice (see Types and conditions of service and Conscription).

4.3.5 One conscript was reported by several sources to have been sentenced to death for refusing orders to shoot protesters during nationwide protests in January 2026. In addition, a Norway-based human rights organisation reported that ‘dozens’ of security forces in Kermanshah Province were arrested in January 2026 for refusing to fire on protesters. Beyond this, there is no evidence to indicate that conscripts are likely to be involved in acts contrary to the basic rules of human conduct whilst performing military service in Iran (see State treatment and, for information about what amounts to ‘the basic rules of human conduct’, see the Asylum Instruction, Military Service and Conscientious Objection).

4.3.6 For guidance on 1F see the Asylum Instruction Exclusion: Article 1F of the Refugee Convention, and on assessing risk, the Asylum Instructions Assessing Credibility and Refugee Status and Military Service and Conscientious Objection.

4.4 Conditions in military service

4.4.1 Conscripts are unlikely to face conditions and/or treatment while serving their military service that would, including by virtue of its nature or repetition, be so harsh as to amount to a real risk of serious harm. The onus is on the person to demonstrate otherwise.

4.4.2 Conditions for conscripts vary with the type and place of deployment. Impartial information on the treatment and working conditions of conscripts is limited. However, conditions in the Revolutionary Guard are reported to be better than in the regular Army. Salaries, which, the law states, are paid in addition to the provision of living facilities, clothing, food, health, and medical care, are relatively low; although they are reported by the Iranian government to have increased significantly over recent years. Monthly salaries for soldiers were reported to range between approximately £30 and £135, representing 60% to 90% of the minimum salary for regular personnel of the armed forces. A further 20% salary increase was reported for 2026, however, CPIT has been unable to confirm whether the increase was implemented. The average monthly salary in Iran, in late 2023, was between £112 and £187 (applying 2026 exchange rates to the amounts reported in Iranian currency) (see Types and conditions of service).

4.4.3 Acts of humiliation, leave restrictions, and poor sanitation during military training are reported by one Kurdish news agency. Another media outlet reported that in March 2026, during international conflict in Iran, frontline units operated with minimal ammunition, food, and other basic supplies, and that access to medical assistance for injured military personnel was restricted. A small number of suicides or violent acts, allegedly due to physical or psychological pressures of military service, were reported since 2023, though one media outlet reported that approximately 15% of soldiers have suicidal thoughts (see Types and conditions of service).

4.4.4 See also the Asylum Instructions on Assessing Credibility and Refugee Status and Military Service and Conscientious Objection.

4.5 Draft evasion or desertion

4.5.1 A military service evader or deserter is unlikely to be subjected to a punishment that, including by virtue of its nature or repetition, amounts to serious harm. The onus is on the person to demonstrate otherwise.

4.5.2 The available evidence does not indicate a harshening of treatment towards draft evaders/ deserters during periods of international conflicts and/or civil unrest such as national protests.

4.5.3 A person who evades military service by leaving the country remains liable to be drafted on return until the age of 50, although for those over the age of 40, a fine is the most common punishment. Draft evaders may also lose social benefits and civil rights including access to employment and higher education, and the ability to obtain a driver’s licence or passport, or to leave the country. In June 2025, during a period of international conflict in Iran, the Supreme Leader reportedly warned that any act of desertion or disobedience would be treated as treason, punishable by execution. However, the Iranian government has also announced several regulations which pardon some deserters, retrospectively exempting them from military service under specified circumstances. These include absence for more than 8 years, or more than 2 months where the deserter developed physical or mental health issues post-desertion, and absentees who qualify for veteran or caregiving exemptions (see Draft evaders and desertion).

4.5.4 Draft evaders and deserters who remain liable for prosecution may, under the law, face imprisonment. One academic source indicated that Kurds and other ethnic minorities could receive harsher punishments than Persians, due to a higher likelihood of being perceived by the state as political opponents. Penalties under the law during wartime are more severe than in peacetime. Combatants who desert from the front line, causing the Islamic front to fail or resulting in casualties to Iranian forces, shall be treated as mohareb (a person who commits moharebeh, defined as drawing a weapon on the life, property or chastity of people or to cause terror as it creates the atmosphere of insecurity) according to the military and armed service laws. Moharebeh can invoke a punishment of the death penalty, at the discretion of the judge. While it was reported that one conscript  was sentenced to death, and ‘dozens’ of security forces in Kermanshah Province were arrested, for refusing orders to shoot protesters during the nationwide protests of January 2026 (see also Acts contrary to the basic rules of human conduct), CPIT has been unable to find any evidence of evaders or deserters having been prosecuted since 2023, including during periods of international conflict and national unrest in Iran in 2025 and 2026. This should be considered in context of reports that there are between one and 4 million deserters in Iran (see Bibliography, Draft evaders and desertion, Conditions and treatment, and Buy-outs).

4.5.5. See also the Asylum Instructions on Assessing Credibility and Refugee Status and Military Service and Conscientious Objection.

8. Legal context

8.1 Constitution

8.1.1 The Iran Data Portal, an online portal which hosts social science data on Iran in both English and Farsi[footnote 1] (Persian), published an English translation of the Constitution of the Islamic Republic of Iran, which states: ‘Article 3 (State Goals) In order to attain the objectives … [of the Islamic Republic of Iran,] the government of the Islamic Republic of Iran has the duty of directing all its resources to … [among other goals] 11) all round strengthening of the foundations of national defence to the utmost degree by means of universal military training for the sake of safeguarding the independence, territorial integrity, and the Islamic order of the country …’[footnote 2]

8.1.2 Chapter 9, Section 3 of the Constitution relates to the army and the Islamic Revolution Guards Corps (IRGC) and states:

‘Article 143 (Army Functions) The Army of the Islamic Republic of Iran is responsible for guarding the independence and territorial integrity of the country, as well as the order of the Islamic Republic.

‘Article 144 (Islamic Army) The Army of the Islamic Republic of Iran must be an Islamic Army, i.e., committed to Islamic ideology and the people, and must recruit into its service individuals who have faith in the objectives of the Islamic Revolution and are devoted to the cause of realizing its goals.

‘… Article 147 (Peace Functions) In time of peace, the government must utilize the personnel and technical equipment of the Army in relief operations, and for educational and productive ends, and the Construction Jihad while fully observing the criteria of Islamic justice and ensuring that such utilization does not harm the combat-readiness of the Army.

‘Article 148 (No Personal Use) All forms of personal use of military vehicles, equipment, and other means, as well as taking advantage of Army and chauffeurs or [sic] [for]bidden.

‘Article 149 (Promotions) Promotions in military rank and their withdrawal take place in accordance with the law.

‘Article 150 (Islamic Revolution Guards Corps) The Islamic Revolution Guards Corps, organized in the early days of the triumph of the Revolution, is to be maintained so that it may continue in its role of guarding the Revolution and its achievements. The scope of the duties of this Corps, and its areas of responsibility, in relation to the duties and areas of responsibility of the other Armed Forces, are to be determined by law with emphasis on brotherly cooperation and harmony among them.

‘Article 151 (Military Training) In accordance with … Koranic verse … the government is obliged to provide a program of military training, with all requisite facilities, for all its citizens, in accordance with the Islamic criteria, in such a way that all citizens will always be able to engage in the armed defence of the Islamic Republic of Iran. The possession of arms, however, requires the granting of permission by the competent authorities.’[footnote 3]

9. Armed forces structure

9.1.1 Under articles 107 and 110 of the Constitution of the Islamic Republic of Iran, the Supreme Leader has ultimate command of the Armed Forces.[footnote 12]

9.1.2 On 30 January 2023, Foreign Policy (FP), a US-based magazine which ‘showcases the smartest ideas and analysis on global affairs by experts, journalists, and practitioners of international relations’[footnote 13] published an article which stated:

‘Since the 1979 Islamic Revolution, Iran has had a dual-military structure, split between the regular armed forces (the Artesh) and the clerical regime’s ideological army (the IRGC). Whereas the former operates as a conventional nation-state army mandated to protect Iran’s territorial integrity, the latter is a nonconventional force that is constitutionally mandated to enforce and spread the hard-line Islamist ideology of the regime at home and abroad through an “ideological mission of jihad in God’s path.”’[footnote 14]

9.1.3 A ‘Country Focus’ report, published by the European Union Asylum Agency (EUAA) in June 2024, citing various sources, stated:

‘The Iranian armed forces are made up of three main branches: the Artesh, which is Iran’s traditional or regular military force, Sepah-e pasdaran-e enqelab-e eslami known as the Islamic Revolutionary Guards Corps (IRGC), and the Law Enforcement Command of the Islamic Republic of Iran (FARAJA [‘formerly known as the Law Enforcement Force of the Islamic Republic of Iran (NAJA)’.[footnote 15] The Army and the IRGC were created on the basis of “faith and ideology”, according to the country’s constitution. The Artesh is tasked to secure territorial integrity, while the IRGC is mandated to preserve the Islamic Republic since its establishment in 1979 by the former Supreme Leader, Ayatollah Khomeini. In 1988, Iranian authorities created the Armed Forces General Staff (AFGS) to liaise between the Artesh and IRGC. The AFGS is the highest military institution in Iran, “which implements policy and coordinates and monitors activities across services.” The IRGC operates as a parallel army to the Artesh. 10. Conscription

10.1 Eligibility

10.1.1 The Ekhtebar publication of the Public Service Duty Law states:

‘Article 2 - Any male Iranian who reaches the age of eighteen in a given month is subject to the rules of compulsory military service during that month.

‘Note – A person subject to military service is someone who falls under the provisions of [this,] the compulsory military service law.

‘… Article 4 …

‘… Note 1 - The start and end dates of service in the reserve and precautionary stages are calculated from the end date of mandatory service, and in any case, the end date will not exceed the age of 50.

‘Note 2 - In times of necessity and general mobilization, individuals subject to this law may also be called up until the age of 60.’[footnote 31] CPIT was, however, unable to find any information in the sources consulted (see Bibliography) to indicate that anyone between the ages of 50 and 60 have been called up for mandatory military service (looking at the period since 2023). This lack of evidence should be considered in the context of the 2025 and 2026 international conflicts inside Iran, and a period of national unrest in December 2025/ January 2026.

11. Exemptions

11.1 Provisions

11.1.1 Article 1 of the Public Service Duty Law (see also Military and armed service laws) states that, ‘… no one subject to compulsory military service can be exempted from it except in cases explicitly stated in this law.’[footnote 89]

11.1.2 Chapter 4 of the Public Service Duty Law covers exemptions, with part 1 covering educational exemptions, part 2 covering medical exemptions, and part 3 covering medical exemptions, and part 3 covering ‘caregiving’ exemptions.[footnote 90]

11.1.3 Article 11 of the Public Service Duty Law states: ‘In times of war and general mobilization, if necessary, those who were exempted from mandatory service during peacetime for any reason will also be called to service and will receive the same benefits and rights as those in their reserve class.’[footnote 91]

11.1.4 The February 2023 IRBC response stated: ‘Sources report that in cases of wartime mobilization, an individual exempted from military service “can” be “called to service” or will be “required to report for duty”. The Associate Fellow added that the individual could have their service exemption card “revoked” in such circumstances.’[footnote 92]

11.1.5 The April 2023 IRNA article stated: ‘The law grants exemptions based on an individual’s age or medical conditions, among other factors. The exemptions, however, are mostly only valid in peacetime and those exempted would be called up for duty in case of a wartime mobilization.’[footnote 93] CPIT was, however, unable to find any information in the sources consulted (see Bibliography) to indicate that people previously exempted from military service were called up, during the 2025 or 2026 conflicts in Iran.

11.3Medical grounds

11.3.1 The Ekhtebar publication of the Public Service Duty Law tates:

‘Article 39 – Conscripts are classified into four categories based on physical condition and mental and physical aptitude, as follows:

‘1 - Those who are physically and mentally healthy and capable of performing service.

‘2 - Those who are not fully healthy due to a physical defect or illness but are capable of performing non-combat service.

‘3 - Those who are temporarily unable to perform compulsory service due to developmental issues or illness.

‘4 - Conscripts who are permanently unable to perform compulsory service due to a physical defect or illness, either physical or mental.

‘… Article 40 - Individuals who are temporarily unable to serve in mandatory military service due to illness will be given a temporary medical exemption for the period determined by the Medical Council, which in any case will not exceed one year. In the second year, if the illness continues, the same procedure will be followed, and if in the third year they are still unable to serve, they will be permanently exempt from mandatory military service.

‘… Note 2 - In times of war or general mobilization, individuals who were exempt from mandatory military service in peacetime due to medical reasons or dependency can also be called to service.

‘Article 41 - All conscripts must be examined by a physician from the armed forces before being sent for service. If there is no physician from the armed forces, they should be examined by civilian doctors who are committed and whose qualifications have been approved by the police force.’[footnote 101]

11.3.2 Note 1 to Article 39 of the Public Service Duty Law notes that medical exemption criteria are set out in the Regulations for Medical Examination and Exemption of Compulsory Military Service Conscripts, Approved in 2014, published by Ekhtebar on 1 March 2015.[footnote 102]

‘Article 48 - Conscripts, based on their educational qualifications and the availability of organizational positions, after completing the necessary training period, achieve one of the following ranks:

‘1 - Conscripts without a high school diploma reach one of the ranks from private to corporal.

‘2 - Conscripts with a high school diploma reach one of the ranks of sergeant or combatant.

‘3 - Conscripts with an associate degree reach one of the ranks of warrant officer, combatant officer, or second lieutenant.

‘4 - Conscripts with a bachelor’s degree reach the rank of first lieutenant.

‘5 - Conscripts with a master’s degree or higher reach the rank of captain.

‘Note 1: Reaching the ranks of Third Sergeant and higher requires completing the necessary specialized training course and possessing the required qualifications.

12.2 Conditions and treatment

12.2.1 The Ekhtebar publication of the Public Service Duty Law states:

‘Article 49 - The pay of conscripted soldiers is at least sixty percent (60%) and at most ninety percent (90%) of the minimum salary of armed forces personnel under the Civil Service Management Law, which is determined by the General Staff according to their education and ranks and is approved by the Commander-in-Chief. 

‘Note 1 - Married conscripts or those who are heads of households receive an allowance equal to one hundred percent (100%) of the family allowance given to permanent armed forces personnel. 

‘Note 2 - During training and before receiving a rank or shoulder badge, conscripts receive fifty percent (50%) of the payment they will get in their first month of service. 

‘Note 3 - At the end of the compulsory service period, conscripts are paid an amount equal to one month of their salary as a bonus. 

‘Note 4 - Retirement benefits and the salary of the first month of service are not deducted from the monthly salaries of conscripted personnel.

‘Article 49 bis - The armed forces, affiliated organizations, and other agencies that have conscripted personnel are obliged, considering approved budgets, to provide appropriate living facilities, clothing, food, health, and medical care for them at their place of service …

‘… Article 50 - Officers and conscripted non-commissioned officers [NCOs], if sent on a mission, transferred, or serving in deprived, harsh weather areas, or operational zones, are entitled to the same special regulations and benefits as regular officers and permanent NCOs with respect to their salary. Other conscripted personnel in these cases will receive two-thirds of the special allowance and benefits of a regular second sergeant.

‘… Article 51 - Personnel on duty who participate in combat operations will receive an operational allowance according to the regulations prepared by the Ministries of Defense and the Revolutionary Guards and approved by the Supreme Defense Council.’[footnote 143]

13. Draft evaders and desertion

13.1 Penalties under the law

13.1.1 Book 2 of the Civil Code of the IRI, originally published on 23 May 1928, outlines provisions concerning nationality. It states: ‘Article 988 - Iranian nationals cannot abandon their nationality except on the following conditions … 4 - That they have completed their national military service.’[footnote 175]

13.1.2 Chapter 7 of the Public Service Duty Law, published by Ekhtebar, covers the ‘Absence of Those Subject to Mandatory Military Service and Offenses and Penalties’ (translated into English).[footnote 176] It states:

‘Article 58 – Those subject to mandatory military service who are summoned for review or for dispatch to fulfill the mandatory service and do not appear within the specified time or deadline, as well as those who have received temporary exemptions and do not present themselves for review within one month after the expiration of their exemption, are considered absent. Once they present themselves or are apprehended, if they are deemed fit and allowed to serve according to the regulations of this law, they will be sent to service and treated as follows:

‘1 - Those whose initial absence period is up to three months during peacetime and up to fifteen days during wartime will receive three additional months of service. If their initial absence exceeds three months during peacetime or fifteen days during wartime, they will be punished with six additional months of service.

‘2 - Those whose initial absence period exceeds one year during peacetime and two months during wartime, in addition to the extra service mentioned in item (1), are considered to have deserted and will be referred to the competent judicial authorities.

‘Note 1- Absentee conscripts who are sent to service, if they show good behavior, ethics, and diligence in performing their duties during service, or demonstrate bravery and self-sacrifice in combat operations, to the extent that it is certified by the relevant commanders and superiors, according to the regulations announced by the General Staff and with the approval of senior military authorities or their equivalents in the organization, may be exempted from performing additional service, or a part of it.

‘… Note 3 - The situation of conscripts who have a valid excuse for their absence will be addressed according to a regulation that will be announced after approval by the Commander-in-Chief.

‘Note 4 - Regarding the absence of reservist and backup conscripts, the procedures will follow instructions prepared by the General Staff and announced after approval by the Commander-in-Chief.

‘… Article 60 – Anyone who, by committing acts such as forging identity cards, seals, signatures, military service completion cards, exemption cards, using someone else’s identity, influencing others, giving false testimony, providing false certificates, hiding the truth, taking bribes, or deceiving others, enables themselves or others to be exempt from military service, will be prosecuted in a competent court. Considering the conditions and capabilities of the offender, the frequency and seriousness of the crime, and the level of discipline required, they will be sentenced to admonishment, reprimand, or imprisonment from one to five years.’[footnote 177]

13.1.3 Articles 61, 63, and 63-bis of the Public Service Duty Law, and Article 4 of the Law on the Addition of Articles to the Law on Public Service, published by Ekhtebar, set out the penalties for people who either assist another person to be falsely exempt from military service or who employ a person who is considered to be absent from compulsory military service.

13.1.4 The Law on the Addition of Articles to the Law on Public Service states:

‘Article 2 - The punishment for absent conscripts during wartime who are caught a second time, in addition to the punishment provided in Article 58 of the Public Duty Service, will be one to three years of suspended imprisonment, and in any case, they are required to fulfill their public duty service.

‘Article 3 - The duration of compulsory service for wartime absent conscripts who present themselves three months after the approval of this letter will be three months longer than for other conscripts, and for those who are arrested, it will be six months longer. It is clear that absent conscripts who present themselves within three months after the approval of this law will not have to serve extra time.’[footnote 178]

13.1.5 On 15 July 2013, the Iran Human Rights Documentation Centre (IHRDC), a non-profit organisation of human rights scholars and lawyers, which focuses on human rights in Iran[footnote 179], published an English translation of Book Five of the ‘new’ Islamic Penal Code, which deals with deals with ta’zir crimes (crimes with discretionary punishments[footnote 180] and deterrent punishments, crimes against national security and a wide range of other offences.[footnote 181] It states: ‘Article 504 – Anyone who effectively encourages combatants or those in military forces to rebel, escape, surrender, or disobey military orders, with the intention to overthrow the government or to defeat national forces against the enemy, shall be considered as mohareb; otherwise (if he does not possess the intention) if his acts are effective he shall be sentenced to two to ten years, and if not, to six months to three years’ imprisonment.’[footnote 182]

13.1.6 Book Two of the Islamic Penal Code, an English translation of which was published by IHRDC on 4 April 2014, defines a mohareb/ moharebeh, and sets out the punishments that may be handed down for mohareb by law:

‘Article 279 – Moharebeh is defined as drawing a weapon on the life, property or chastity of people or to cause terror as it creates the atmosphere of insecurity. When a person draws a weapon on one or several specific persons because of personal enmities and his act is not against the public, and also a person who draws a weapon on people, but, due to inability does not cause insecurity, shall not be considered as a mohareb (i.e. a person who commits moharebeh).

‘… Article 282 – The hadd [an Islamic crime for which the Quran specifies the punishment[footnote 183]] punishment for moharebeh is one of the following four punishments:

‘(a) The death penalty (hanging)

‘(b) Crucifixion

‘(c) Amputation of right hand and left foot

‘(d) Banishment [of at least one year, where the mohareb has repented[footnote 184]]

‘Article 283 – The judge has the discretion of choosing one of the four punishments prescribed in article 282.’[footnote 185]

13.1.7 Chapter 2 of the Law on Punishment of Armed Forces Crimes, published by Ekhtebar, covers crimes against internal and external security, and states: ‘Article 23 – Any military personnel who force or incite soldiers or individuals serving in the armed forces in any way to escape, surrender, or neglect their military duties, or facilitate escape, or knowingly hide those who are on the run, if done with the intent to overthrow the government or weaken their own forces against the enemy, will be punished as a high-level combatant with imprisonment ranging from three to fifteen years.’[footnote 186]

N.B. the information quoted above, and all other COI quoted from this source throughout the rest of this CPIN, was originally published in Farsi (Persian). See About the country information for further information about the method of translation.

13.1.8 Chapter 4 of the Law on Punishment of Armed Forces Crimes addresses desertion from service, with section one of chapter 4 covering desertion during peacetime:

‘Article 56 – Permanent members of the armed forces, if they are absent for more than fifteen consecutive days during peacetime without a valid excuse, will be considered deserters and, depending on the case, will be subject to the following penalties:

‘(a) If they turn themselves in, they may be sentenced to two to six months in prison or deprived of promotion for three to six months.

‘(b) If they are caught, they may be sentenced to six months to two years in prison or deprived of promotion for six months to two years.

‘… Article 58 – Permanent members of the Armed Forces who are deserters, if during wartime, mobilization, general call-up, or when their unit is on combat readiness, report within one month from the date war, mobilization, general call-up, or combat readiness is announced, will be exempt from prosecution and punishment. If the report is made after this period, or if they are caught during or after this period, it will be considered desertion during wartime.

‘Article 59 – Armed forces conscripts who are absent for more than fifteen consecutive days during peacetime without a valid excuse are considered deserters, and if caught, they are sentenced to imprisonment from three months to one year or to three months to one year of additional service.

‘Article 60 – Deserter conscripts during peacetime, if they voluntarily report and resume their service, will be treated as follows:

‘a – If it is their first time deserting and they return within sixty days from the start of their absence, without referring the case to the judicial authority, two days of mandatory service will be added for each day of absence and desertion. This additional service will not exceed three months. 

‘b – If they report after the period mentioned in clause (a) of this article, or if they have a history of desertion, they will be sentenced by the court to imprisonment from two to six months.’[footnote 187]

13.1.9 Deserter conscripts under article 60 will be exempt from prosecution and punishment if they report for service within 2 months from the start of war, mobilisation, general call-up, or a declaration of combat readiness (after 2 months they will be considered a deserter in wartime).[footnote 188] Additionally, any service days added under article 60 may be excused if a committee of commanders, political-religious officers, and security officers or their deputies (at a requisite level) decide that a justified excuse for desertion has been provided, or where they approve the completion of the remaining service (also with the approval of commanders).[footnote 189]

13.1.10 Part 2 of chapter 4 of the ‘Law on Punishment of Armed Forces Crimes’, covers desertion during war and armed conflicts. It states:

‘Article 61 – Permanent members of the armed forces, if absent for more than five consecutive days during wartime without a valid excuse, shall be considered deserters and after being captured will be sentenced to imprisonment from one to five years. If the desertion is from the front, it will be counted from the time of absence and they will be sentenced to imprisonment from three to fifteen years.

‘Article 62 – The deserters mentioned in Article 61 of this law, if they present themselves voluntarily and resume service, if their desertion occurred during wartime service, shall be sentenced to imprisonment from one to three years and if the desertion was from the front, they shall be sentenced to two to five years of imprisonment.

‘Article 63 – Conscripts of the armed forces, if absent for more than five consecutive days during wartime without a valid excuse, shall be considered deserters and after being captured will be sentenced to imprisonment from one to three years. If the desertion is from the front, it will be counted from the time of absence and they will be sentenced to imprisonment from two to five years.

‘Article 64 – Those who have deserted as mentioned in Article 63 of this law, if they personally come forward and start serving, in case their desertion happened from service during wartime, they will be sentenced to imprisonment from three months to one year, and if the desertion was from the front, to imprisonment from six months to two years.

‘Note – The term “front” in this law refers to an area where a unit has had direct and fast combat engagement or has been assigned such a mission.

‘Article 65 – If the desertion of the individuals mentioned in Articles 61, 62, 63, and 64 of this law causes the Islamic front to fail or results in casualties to our own forces, they shall be sentenced as moharebs (enemies of God).

‘… Article 70 – Those who escape and are subject to Articles (62) and (64) of this law can only use the reduction provided in these articles the first time they desert service. In case of multiple or repeated desertions, they will be punished according to Articles (61) and (63) of this law, as applicable.’[footnote 190]

13.1.11 Article 57 of the Law on Punishment of Armed Forces Crimes, referring to permanent armed forces members, and article 67, referring to ‘military personnel’, note that those who are on assignment, leave, or training courses, and who do not report for duty (in or outside of Iran, as relevant) within 15 days (during peacetime, article 57) or 5 days (during wartime, article 67) after the end of their assignment, leave or training course, without a valid excuse, will be considered deserters and subject to punishment under articles 56 and articles 61 to 64, respectively.[footnote 191]

13.1.12 Additional provisions of the armed forces crimes punishment law are:

‘Article 68 – Any military personnel who deserts during mobilization or when their unit is on combat alert will, depending on the case, be subject to the punishments set out in this section.

‘Article 69 – Reservists and people in the reserve duty who are called up during wartime and fail to report themselves within five days from the date of summons without a valid excuse will be considered deserters and sentenced to imprisonment from six months to two years. If these individuals report themselves before the end of the war, the court will reduce their punishment.’[footnote 192]

13.1.13 Part 3 of chapter 4 of the armed forces crimes punishment law sets out what qualifies as a ‘justifiable excuse’ and the procedure for military personnel to follow in such cases (article 76), the period of absence punishable where it is partially covered by a justified excuse (article 77), and the penalties for military personnel who: use weapons, vehicles, or other equipment used by the armed forces to affect their escape; defect to the enemy and contribute to the defeat of the Islamic Republic; escape with conspiracy (i.e. more than 2 personnel who collude to escape together); are students or ‘contractual members’ performing contractual service within the military under ‘Armed Forces Employment Regulations’, or serve within the Special Basij (articles 71 to 75). See part 3 of chapter 4 of the Law on Punishment of Armed Forces Crimes for details.

13.1.14 Regarding the IRGC specifically, Iran Data Portal published an English translation of the ‘Constitution of the Revolutionary Guards’ dated 7 September 1982 which states: ‘Article 46: Should a member of the Revolutionary Guard be absent without leave for over fifteen days in peacetime or five days in wartime, he shall be considered a deserter and shall be pursued by the military courts and subject to punishment under the law. The punishment for desertion shall be less than the punishment for desertions according to the disciplinary regulations ratified by the Revolutionary Guards’ Supreme Council.’[footnote 193]

13.1.15 The November 2023 Iran International article stated: ‘Under Iran’s constitution, all men over 18 years of age are required to complete approximately two years of military service. 13.1.16 The IHRNGO and ECPM 2024 and 2025 death penalty reports, the latter having been published on 13 April 2026, both stated: ‘Of the death penalty charges most commonly leading to execution, the security-related charges of moharebeh [see paragraph 13.1.6], efsad-fil-arz [corruption on earth[footnote 195]], [and] baghy [rebellion[footnote 196]] … fall under the jurisdiction of the Revolutionary Courts … Military courts also have jurisdiction over death penalty charges restricted to times of war, according to the Code of Criminal Procedure for Armed Forces Offences and Electronic Proceedings.’[footnote 197] [footnote 198]

‘Carrying out the following matters regarding those subject to compulsory service requires providing proof of review of their conscription status by the Public Service Office, and a photocopy of the submitted document is among the essential attachments of the formed file:

‘b - Receiving any kind of loans and assistance for agriculture, industry, livestock, and housing through banks, ministries, government institutions, and government-affiliated legal entities.

The penalty for such individuals is, for the first time, paying the annual cost of one soldier according to the annual budget law and the rate announced by the government; for the second time, in addition to the above fine, three months of suspension from service; and for the third time and beyond, dismissal from government service.’[footnote 199]

These individuals are employed in the IRGC in their city and province of residence, and at the same time as serving in the military, they can work and also have insurance coverage.’[footnote 203]

13.2.7 On 17 October 2024, the EUAA published a response to a request for information on the human rights situation in Iran, which covered the period from January 2023 until 7 October 2024 and cited various sources. Citing an article published on 13 May 2024 by Eghtesad (also transliterated as Eqtesad) Online, an Iranian media group[footnote 204], entitled ‘What’s the new law about exemptions for deserter soldiers?’[footnote 205], which was published in Farsi, the EUAA response stated (in English):

‘General Taghi Mehri, head of Iran’s General Military Service Organisation, has reportedly announced new regulations for conscripts and deserters in 2024, including:

  • ‘Exemption for Deserters: deserters who have been absent for more than 8 years (calculated until 2016) and have not resumed service are eligible for exemption from military service. This aims to ease their financial and social burdens and grant them the same benefits as those who completed their service. Deserters with less than 8 years of absence can finish their service in intervals based on personal circumstances;
  • ‘Medical Exemption for Deserters: Deserters absent for more than two months who developed physical or mental health issues post-desertion may qualify for a medical exemption after paying a fine. The process requires reporting to the last unit served, followed by legal proceedings and payment of fines. Upon approval by a medical board, they may receive a medical exemption at no extra cost;
  • ‘Caregiving Exemption (Kefalat): deserters can apply for caregiving exemptions (for parents, siblings, etc.) only if they had no unjustified absence before conscription. After completing military court procedures and paying fines, their exemption can be confirmed, allowing them to return to their unit;
  • ‘Veteran Exemption: deserters whose fathers were not previously recognised for wartime service or disability can now qualify for veteran-based exemptions. After desertion, once documents proving the father’s service or disability are submitted, deserters can return to their unit, settle legal matters, and apply for service reductions or a permanent exemption via the police service.’[footnote 206]

13.2.8 The same EUAA response, citing an article published on 23 June 2024 by Tasnim News, a hardline news agency close to the IRGC[footnote 207], which CPIT has been unable to access directly, stated:

‘… (informal translation) “Amir Hussein Qazi Zadeh, presidential candidate, stated that there were 3.5 million military deserters in the country. The source further indicated that (informal translation) “these individuals are not deserters but rather conscripts who, for physical related issues or medical exemption validation or obtaining a smart card, were not present at military service. The source also indicated that there have been less than one million absent conscripts in the country”. Corroborating information could not be found in the sources consulted by the EUAA.’[footnote 208]

13.2.9 The June 2024 ISNA article stated: ‘

‘The head of the Judicial Organization of Armed Forces in Kermanshah said: On the occasion of the Eid al-Adha [generally considered the holier of the 2 annual Islamic Eid festivals[footnote 209]] and Ghadir Khumm [one of the most significant Shia annual events, commemorating the appointment of the first Shia imam[footnote 210]] festivals and Judiciary Week, deserters who turn themselves in will be exempt from punishment.

‘On June 23 [2025] — the 12th day of the [12-day[footnote 216]] war between the Islamic Republic of Iran and Israel — Tehran-based Farda News [a news website that is ‘… affiliated to the Progress and Justice Society of Islamic Iran, a conservative party which is close to Majlis Speaker Mohammad Baqer Qalibaf.’[footnote 217] reported that senior officials from Iran’s Armed Forces General Staff had granted local commanders the authority to take severe measures against any cases of desertion or disobedience among soldiers and officers across all branches of the security forces [CPIT has been unable to access the Farda News report directly].

All members of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC), Basij forces, and Law Enforcement Forces are reminded that any act of disobedience to their unit’s orders or regulations puts their own lives at risk and could expose their families to similar accusations,” it said.

‘The signs are that the Islamic Republic has once again turned to espionage and informant tactics, seeking to turn citizens against one another during a time of war.

‘“The public is urged by intelligence agencies and the Ministry of Intelligence to report instances of desertion or disobedience. Such information is crucial to the Republic’s security and ensuring our triumph over the enemy,” the report added.’[footnote 218]
